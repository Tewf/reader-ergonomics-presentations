# Evidence against and open controversies

This file gathers the evidence against the presentation rules: null results, disputed studies, and advice that a study contradicts, by theme.

## Against assertion-evidence

- One experiment carries most of the weight: Garner 2013 (n = 110, one topic, one 8-minute talk, engineering students).
- The coauthor Alley is the main designer and promoter of the approach.
- The owner's site says it makes talks more understandable and cites no study on its front page (Leonhard Center 2026, 9 of 20).
- Other tests are not clean: Alley 2007 bundles the design with active learning and shares an author with Garner 2013; Root Kustritz 2014 let students choose their session.
- The arms differ in words per slide (21.2 against 41.5) and in graphics; the design is a package, so the cause is unknown.
- AE viewers rated their slides as having a less appropriate amount of text than the other group did (Garner 2013).
- Immediate fact recall did not differ; the gain was in explanation and delayed items.
- Later studies measured other things: slide makers' workload (Alzayed 2023), grammar scores (Sripradith 2023), and pilots (Wolfe 2023).

## Against the principles as rules for talks

- Most principle studies are short narrated lessons on a computer; a live talk adds a person, a room and questions.
- Redundancy effects depend on prior knowledge and picture content (Adesope 2012); "never put text on the slide" overreaches.
- The modality effect can reverse under self-pacing (secondary); a talk is system-paced, so that result fits it better than it fits a page.
- Segmenting helped more for high prior knowledge in one account (secondary).
- Seductive details hurt (Sundararajan 2020), yet Wolfe 2023 reports that organiser graphics such as SmartArt can backfire, which supports testing images with an audience.

## Against the critics of slides

- Tufte 2003 argues the tool is the fault; his case is an essay with an accident report as its example, not a trial.
- CAIB 2003 is an investigation of an organisation; slides were one of several factors in what it found. The page numbers 191 and 201 came from a secondary page.
- Baker 2018 shows no average harm either, so "slides are bad" is not supported.
- Kosslyn 2012 shows violations are common, not that fixing them raises learning.

## Against the attention rule

- The belief that attention drops after 10 to 15 minutes has little primary support (Wilson 2007, Bradbury 2016).
- Short fixed formats (Ignite, five minutes) cannot cite it as evidence.

## Against practice formats

- Liao 2020: the differences between PechaKucha and a traditional talk mostly reflected the selection of less material to fit the time, not the format.
- Bakcek 2020 (randomised, n = 134): equal test scores; only satisfaction was higher.
- Warmuth 2021: instructors rated PechaKucha higher, but delayed retention did not differ for presenters; audience retention was self-reported.

## Against accessibility advice and the redundancy result

- Guides ask speakers to say every slide word aloud (Rector 2019; ACM 2026), which is the redundancy that Adesope 2012 and Yue 2013 found unhelpful for sighted learners.
- Rector 2019 itself allows more text on slides when the speaker talks fast. No study of blind or low-vision listeners on this trade-off was found.

## Against the speaker-effect claims

- A visible face has no consistent effect over 12 studies (Henderson 2021).
- Enthusiasm raised engagement but not quiz scores (Marty-Dugas 2024).
- Agent studies use animated agents, not live speakers (Castro-Alonso 2021).

## Against motion on slides and in talks

- The build studies are small and recorded: n = 99 and n = 40, one lesson each (Liu 2022, Ito 2026); one course found exams unaffected by animated slides (Miller 2011).
- Learner control beat system pacing on transfer (Tabbers 2009), but cost time; a speaker-paced build is the system-paced arm.
- A whiteboard animation raised satisfaction and not knowledge, and its authors suspect seductive details (Meij 2021); engaged viewers comprehended slightly less (Li 2023, secondary).
- A slides-only video matched an animation, at lower cost (Pichler 2023).
- Watching time is not learning (Guo 2014); its 6-minute rule comes from engagement logs in four edX courses (computer science, statistics, AI, chemistry).
- Live coding did not beat static code on learning in one controlled study (Shah 2023). Active live coding did not beat traditional live coding (Shah 2025). The engagement gain is not a learning gain.
- Accessibility guides ask for no animation (Rector 2019); the WCAG rule is only to allow pausing (W3C 2023b).

## Against colour claims

- Emotional design: the colour-only subgroup is 5 of 33 samples (Brom 2018; manuscript, not rechecked); studies disagree on learning and agree on motivation (Mutlu-Bayraktar 2024); designs are too varied to compare (Desiron 2026). Wong 2020's updated values are a little smaller than Brom 2018's.
- Colour as retrieval cue did not help (Meusel 2024); hue-signalled sections helped one test and not another (Clariana 2009).
- Rainbow maps: a model found they can help some inference tasks (Reda 2021, secondary), so the ban is a default, not a law.
- The rule of dark ground for projection has no support in the one experiment found (Greco 2008, secondary).
- Harmony: contrasting hue pairs, the "complementary" ones of the colour wheel, were rated less harmonious (Schloss 2011, secondary); preference is not comprehension.
- Practice cites weakly sourced figures: a 90% claim for colour in quick judgments, citing a study by name without a working link (Ethos3 2026, 2.5 of 20).
- Old colour-versus-monochrome studies were excluded by Brom 2018 because the colours were arbitrary.

## Replications and notes

- Laptop against longhand notes: the quiz difference did not replicate in a direct replication (Urry 2019).
- Handout studies are not randomised (Worthington 2015, full text).
- Practice pages report their own success: Designer, Ignite, PechaKucha, the memo, the assertion-evidence site. Of these, all but the memo (Bezos 2018, 10.5) score under 10 of 20.
