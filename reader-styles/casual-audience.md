# Reader style: casual audience

How the presentation principles change for a room that chose to come and can leave. It has three parts: who the
listener is, the overrides, and what is open.

## Who

- **Listener:** someone at a meetup, a club, a lightning-talk evening, a popular-science talk, or among friends and
  classmates; what a casual talk is, is set out in [the review, section 10](../evidence/presentations/review.md).
- **Expertise:** none assumed; the room is lay or mixed.
- **Medium:** a room, often small, with slides or without.
- **Reading mode:** listening by choice, with the freedom to stop.

## Overrides

No study measures a live casual talk, so most rows below are conventions; each keeps its grade. Everything else in the
[principles](../principles/README.md) holds, the claim headline and legibility in the room included.

| Principle | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| [Define before use](https://github.com/Tewf/reader-ergonomics/blob/v1.11.0/principles/document/define-before-use.md) | strengthen: assume no prior knowledge. Say who the talk is for, define every term before its first use, and prefer a plain word to a technical one | McCarthy 2025 (the audience is seldom defined); Rakedzon 2017 (lay summaries still above the jargon limit). Supported with caveat; no source tests assuming none |
| [A talk is not a paper](../principles/talk/a-talk-is-not-a-paper.md) | change: open with why a non-specialist should care, in the first minute, before any background | Childers 2021 (attendees came for learning and company). Convention, untested |
| [A talk is not a paper](../principles/talk/a-talk-is-not-a-paper.md) | change: in a slot of 3 to 10 minutes, a hard limit, give one idea and no plan slide; the point is said at the start and at the end. A deck declares its slot (`<html data-minutes="5">`), and the talk-structure check then asks for no plan | Bakcek 2020 (equal test scores in a short format); competition rules and guides, all examples. Convention, untested |
| (evidence) | add: a story may carry the content for a lay room: a person, a problem, what happened | Dahlstrom 2014; Zebregs 2015 (health only). Supported with caveat |
| (content) | allow humour or an anecdote only when it is tied to the point; cut unrelated humour like any detail | Zhou 2025 (perceived learning, rapport); Garner 2006 (one experiment: related humour raised recall); Sundararajan 2020. Supported with caveat: affect is clear, learning mixed |
| [Claim headline with evidence](../principles/slide/claim-headline-with-evidence.md) | relax: slides are optional, none, one or a few; when there are slides, their rules hold | Baker 2018 (slides neutral on average). Measured for "neutral on average"; convention for none |
| [Motion adds, never replaces](../principles/pace/motion-adds-never-replaces.md) | keep, and say the risk: a live demo is a choice, with a recorded fallback | Shah 2023 (live code taught no more than static code); guides disagree, all examples. Convention |
| [Plan the questions](../principles/talk/plan-the-questions.md) | change: in a small room, questions may come during the talk, as discussion; the speaker repeats each question | Hunsu 2016 (classes; the questions, not the device, carry the gain); guides, examples. Supported with caveat for classes; convention for talks |

## Thresholds

Unchanged. A casual room still has a back row, so type size and contrast hold.

## Open questions

- Whether any of these overrides changes what a casual audience understands or keeps: no study of a live casual talk
  with a measured outcome was found.
- Whether one idea in a short slot is understood better than two, or only easier to give.
- Whether questions during the talk help the room or only the speaker.
