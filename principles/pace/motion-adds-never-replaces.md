# Motion adds, never replaces

**Claim:** animation, video and live demos are added to an explanation, never in place of it.

- **Grade:** supported with caveat.
- **Evidence:** [review, strand 7](../../evidence/presentations/review.md); [position, Known and Not known](../../evidence/presentations/position.md).
- **Check:** `deck-judgment` (judgment): for each clip, animation or demo, does an explanation come with it?
- **Reader styles:** none changes it.

## What it asks

- Say what the viewer should see before the clip, the animation or the demo starts, and what it showed after.
- Name the learning goal of each visual. A visual without a clear goal can hinder (Bos 2025, abstract).
- Keep embedded clips short, and place an educational clip in the middle of the talk (Ljubojevic 2014, abstract).
- For a live demo, show only what has moving parts, rehearse it, and keep a recording ready (Nederbragt 2016 and LornaJane 2017, conventions).
- Say aloud what you type (Nederbragt 2016, convention).

## Why

In 105 randomised trials (7,776 students), video that replaced other teaching gave g = 0.28 and video added to it gave g = 0.80 (Noetel 2021, abstract).

Animation against static pictures, self-paced, gave d = 0.37 and g = 0.23. The gain was larger when the change is the thing to learn. That result is held in the learning science review, linked from the review above.

## Limits

- In one controlled study, live coding did not teach more than static code (Shah 2023). Active live coding did not beat traditional live coding, though engagement was higher (Shah 2025, abstracts).
- No study of a live demo inside a conference talk was found, and none compares a live demo with a recorded one.
- An animated explainer and a good slide video mostly differ in engagement, not comprehension (Pichler 2023; Li 2023, secondary). Higher engagement went with slightly lower comprehension in Li 2023.
- Students learned from explainer videos and overestimated how much (Bos 2025, about 20 students, no control group).
- Watching time is not learning. The 6-minute rule comes from engagement logs in four edX courses (Guo 2014).
