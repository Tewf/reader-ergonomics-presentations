# Gesture with the words

**Claim:** the speaker gestures at what is said; a visible face adds nothing consistent.

- **Grade:** measured.
- **Evidence:** [review, strand 3](../../evidence/presentations/review.md); [position, Known](../../evidence/presentations/position.md).
- **Check:** `deck-judgment` (judgment): in rehearsal, does the speaker point at what is being discussed?
- **Reader styles:** none changes it; the delivery row of the [business decision maker](../../reader-styles/business-decision-maker.md) is about the pitch, not gesture.

## What it asks

- Point at the figure or the part you discuss, as you name it.
- Draw or trace as you speak when the topic is a process.
- Do not rely on the speaker's face being visible to teach.

## Why

Hostetter 2011 (63 samples) and Dargue 2019 (83 samples) each report a moderate gain in comprehension when gesture goes with speech. Hostetter 2011 found the gain larger for gesture that does not fully repeat the speech, for motor topics and for children.

Drawing as you speak gave d = 0.54, and so did eye contact with the camera. Mere visibility did not help (Fiorella 2019).

## Limits

- A visible instructor has no consistent benefit over 12 studies (Henderson 2021). Strategic and constant face display gave equal learning in a field experiment, N = 12,468 (Kizilcec 2015). Three field experiments on videos over 30 minutes found no effect on learning, none harmful (Heidig 2024).
- A vocally enthusiastic lecturer raised engagement and not quiz scores (Marty-Dugas 2024).
- The agent studies use animated agents, not live speakers. The mean benefit is g about 0.20, and most traits did not moderate it (Castro-Alonso 2021).
- The evidence is from video and short lessons. No review covers a live room.
